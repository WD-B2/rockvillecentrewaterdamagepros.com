How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Rockville Centre

The scope is the contract in plain words. Read it for rooms, materials, quantities and what is left out.

Written by the Rockville Centre Water Damage Pros team · Published October 29, 2026.

Call (516) 845-9582See all services

A scope of work is a written list of what will be done, where and with what materials. In water damage, it usually covers extraction, demolition, drying, cleaning and any rebuild. The scope is where disputes start, so reading it closely pays.

This guide explains how to read one, what each section means and what to ask before you sign.

Read it room by room

A good scope groups work by room or area. It names the room, the surface and the action, for example removing wet carpet pad in the basement or cutting drywall two feet above the floor.

If a scope says only "water damage repair" with a single price, it does not say what is included. Ask for the detail in writing.

Terms you will see

......
TermPlain meaning
Flood cutCutting drywall a set height above the floor to remove wet board and reach the cavity.
Pack-outMoving belongings out so work can happen.
Air moverA fan that pushes air across wet surfaces.
LGR dehumidifierA low grain refrigerant unit that removes water vapor from the air.
AntimicrobialA treatment applied to surfaces after cleaning.
Category 1, 2, 3The contamination level of the water.

These terms come from industry standards such as ANSI/IICRC S500. Ask the contractor to define any term that is unclear.

Check quantities and standards

Quantities matter. Square feet of drywall, linear feet of baseboard and the number of drying units are the figures that make two scopes comparable.

The scope should say how drying is finished. A line that reads "equipment removed when readings reach the dry standard" is better than a fixed number of days. See structural drying for what that looks like.

Gaps to look for

  • Is the water source stopped, and by whom?
  • Is the rebuild included or priced separately?
  • Who removes and disposes of debris?
  • Does the scope cover permits and inspections?
  • How are changes approved?

If a wall is opened and more damage is found, the scope should be updated in writing. Verbal changes lead to arguments later.

Sample lines and what they mean

......
Line in a scopeWhat it means
Extract standing water, living roomPump or vacuum water from that room.
Remove and dispose of wet pad, 400 sq ftThe carpet pad is not salvageable and comes out.
Flood cut drywall 24 inches, north wallWet board is cut to two feet to reach the cavity.
Set 6 air movers and 2 dehumidifiersThe drying equipment placed in the area.
Daily monitoring until dry standardReadings are taken and equipment stays until dry.
Apply antimicrobial to affected surfacesA treatment after cleaning.

The numbers above are examples to show the format, not a quote. A real scope will have its own quantities, and each should match what the contractor measured on site.

What to do when you disagree with a line

If a line looks too large or too small, ask how it was measured. A good contractor can point to a sketch, a photo or a reading. If they cannot, ask for a re-measure before work begins.

Also ask about anything that seems missing. A common gap is a missing line for the source repair, such as the plumber's work after a burst pipe. A scope may leave it out because it belongs to another trade, and you should know that up front.

Keep every version of the scope. When changes happen, ask for a revised copy with a date. If the loss is a commercial one, see commercial water damage restoration for how scopes change when a business must reopen.

Signs of a scope you can trust

A trustworthy scope is specific. It names rooms, surfaces and quantities. It describes how success is measured and what falls outside the job. It leaves room for change and says how changes are approved.

It also matches the photos. If photos show water in three rooms and the scope covers one, ask why. There may be a good reason, such as one room being dry, but you should hear it.

The scope should also be consistent with the invoice. When the final bill arrives, each line should map to something in the scope or to a signed change.

Finally, check dates. The scope should list the start date and the expected duration of drying, with the understanding that readings decide the end. If a scope has no dates at all, ask for them.

Keep a copy of everything

Store the scope, the photos, the readings log and every invoice in one folder. That folder is your file for the insurer, for a later sale and for any dispute.

Ask the contractor for a digital copy of the readings log at the end. It is the proof that the structure was dry when equipment left.

A word about insurance scopes

Insurers write their own scope, often using software that prices common tasks. It may match the contractor's scope closely or differ in several lines, such as the number of drying days or the amount of drywall removed.

When the two differ, a calm conversation with readings and photos usually works best. Ask the contractor to explain the difference in writing so you can pass it along.
