How Do You Get Rid of Mold in a Bathroom? A Rockville Centre Guide

Bathrooms stay damp, so mold returns unless the moisture is handled. Clean the spot, then fix what feeds it.

Written by the Rockville Centre Water Damage Pros team · Published October 19, 2026.

Call (516) 845-9582See all services

Getting rid of mold in a bathroom works best as a two-part job. First clean the visible growth on hard surfaces. Then fix the moisture that let it grow, which is usually poor ventilation, a leaking seal or a slow plumbing leak.

Small spots are within reach of most homeowners. Larger areas, or growth that keeps coming back, point to a leak behind the surface, and New York sets licensing rules for bigger jobs.

Where bathroom mold starts

Grout lines, caulk at the tub, the ceiling above the shower and the wall behind the toilet are the usual places. Steam condenses on cold surfaces and stays there if the fan is weak or never used.

Mold that appears on a wall outside the shower, or under the vanity, often means a leak. A slow drip from a supply line or a failed tub seal feeds growth you cannot see.

Cleaning a small spot

  • Wear gloves, eye protection and an N95 respirator.
  • Scrub tile and grout with detergent and water, then rinse and dry.
  • Remove and replace old caulk that is blackened.
  • Discard porous items such as a shower curtain liner or a bath mat.
  • Run the fan during and after every shower.

Do not mix bleach with other cleaners, which can release dangerous fumes. Do not paint over growth, because paint only hides it.

A bath fan that vents into the attic instead of outside adds moisture to the attic. That is a common hidden cause of mold upstairs, which our guide to attic mold costs touches on.

When it is bigger than a cleaning job

The New York State Department of Labor licenses mold assessors and remediators for jobs over 10 square feet. If growth covers more than that, spreads onto drywall or keeps returning, stop cleaning and get a licensed assessment.

If the leak soaked the wall, wall and drywall water damage repair covers how we dry and cut out wet board. Our mold remediation page explains the licensing in more detail.

Why bathrooms are the first place mold shows up

A bathroom sees daily bursts of warm, humid air in a small space. Tile, grout, caulk, paint and the ceiling above the shower all collect moisture. If the fan is weak, the room stays damp for hours after a shower.

Mold needs a food source, and bathrooms provide plenty. Soap film, skin cells and paper products all feed growth. That is why mold often starts in corners and along caulk lines.

The fix is a combination of cleaning, drying and airflow. Cleaning alone gives a good result for a few weeks if the room is still humid.

A simple routine that keeps it away

  • Run the exhaust fan for at least 20 minutes after each shower.
  • Wipe down wet tile and the glass with a squeegee.
  • Leave the shower curtain spread open to dry.
  • Wash bath mats weekly and hang them to dry.
  • Re-caulk any seam that has gaps or darkened sealant.

If the fan cannot be heard or does not move a sheet of tissue held to the grille, it needs replacing or cleaning. A fan that vents into the attic instead of outdoors creates a different moisture problem upstairs.

When the cause is a plumbing leak, the fix belongs to a plumber first. Afterward, a burst pipe water damage crew can dry the wall cavity so mold does not take hold behind new tile.

Grout, caulk and paint: choosing the right fix

Grout is porous, so it holds mold. Scrub it, let it dry and consider sealing it. If staining remains after cleaning, a grout colorant or regrouting may be the answer.

Caulk fails faster. When it turns black or peels, cutting it out and replacing it is better than cleaning it. Choose a sealant labeled for kitchens and baths, and let it cure before using the shower.

Painted ceilings and walls hold moisture if the paint is old. Clean, dry and repaint with a primer and paint made for bathrooms. If paint bubbles or peels on a wall outside the shower, moisture is coming from inside the wall.

In that case, stop painting. Find the leak and dry the area first.

Protecting yourself while you clean

Wear gloves, eye protection and a well-fitted N95 respirator. Open a window or run the fan, and keep children and pets out of the room until you finish.

Work from the top down so debris falls onto areas you have not cleaned yet. Bag porous waste such as old liners and sponges, seal the bag and put it out with the trash.

Wash your clothes after you finish. If you feel unwell during the job, stop and get fresh air.

Safety steps before you start scrubbing

  • Open a window or run the fan and keep children and pets out.
  • Wear an N95 respirator, gloves and eye protection.
  • Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.
  • Stop if the growth is on drywall or covers more than 10 square feet.
  • Turn off power if water is near outlets or a fixture.

Standards behind the work

The industry references here are ANSI/IICRC S500 for water damage and ANSI/IICRC S520 for mold remediation. In New York, the Department of Labor licenses mold assessors and remediators, so ask any company about the licenses that apply.
